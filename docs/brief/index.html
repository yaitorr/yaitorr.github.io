Policy Brief · Fairfax County Public Schools Board of Education
A Tutor for Every Student
Leveraging AI to Democratize Personalized Learning in Fairfax County Public Schools
Yaisiel O. Torres-Vazquez Purdue University 5447 Calvin Ct, Springfield VA 22151 April 26, 2026
Executive Summary

Core recommendation: Approve a four-part policy package to establish an evidence-based framework for deploying Intelligent Tutoring Systems in FCPS middle schools, prioritizing Title I schools, as a scalable and equitable alternative to general-purpose GenAI in the classroom.

Tutoring has been identified by experts as one of the most impactful pedagogical interventions in a child's education (Nickow et al., 2024) and, therefore, FCPS must act as innovative and cost-efficient modalities arrive. ITS implementations have demonstrated effectiveness in Socratic-method instruction, outperforming more traditional computerized tutoring systems (St-Hilaire et al., 2022, preprint), and hold strong potential for K-12 deployment at scale, capable of replacing systems like STMath and Lexia.

Purposefully designed ITS, compliant with student privacy law, piloted in controlled settings, and evaluated for equity outcomes, represents a responsible and high-value expansion of FCPS's current GenAI policy. Key recommendations include the discrete classification of GenAI tools, the creation of a community advisory committee for AI alignment, a pre-pilot governance framework, and authorization to organize academic partnerships to secure academic expertise and federal grant opportunities. All four are grounded in the policymaking and federally funded action of Washington State's Office of Superintendent of Public Instruction (OSPI) and Washington State University's AmplifyGAIN program, backed by the Institute of Education Sciences (IES). Given the existing tutoring gap and the risk of further isolating students from emerging personalized learning technologies, closing that gap is the board's responsibility, and this policy suite is the path to doing it responsibly.

Introduction

Access to personalized tutoring is one of the most consistently effective interventions in preK-12 education, with a meta-analysis of dozens of randomized experiments finding substantial positive learning effects across grade levels and student populations (Nickow et al., 2024). Yet in Fairfax County, as in most American school systems, high-quality tutoring has remained a private good, accessible primarily to the families with the time and resources to pay for it. Recent advances in Intelligent Tutoring Systems (ITS) offer a scalable, evidence-based path to extending that benefit equitably, at a cost and scope that no prior technology has made possible.

The FCPS Board has signaled its intention to engage with this opportunity. Through its Meeting the Moment initiative, the district has framed AI integration as a strategic priority for teaching, learning, and the future of work (Fairfax County School Board, n.d.). In practice, however, student-facing AI policy has not kept pace with that ambition: no framework currently exists for purpose-built learning tools like ITS (Stout, 2026). This brief argues for a formal classification of AI tools by risk level that recognizes ITS as a distinct and educationally valuable category and provides the board a framework for acting on it. Specifically, it recommends that the board authorize a middle school pilot program with explicit inclusion of Title I schools, supported by a governance structure that addresses FERPA and COPPA compliance, and criteria for scaling. Done well, this is how FCPS can genuinely meet the moment: not by restricting all AI broadly, but by deploying it purposefully where the evidence is strongest and the equity need is greatest.

Problem Statement

High-quality tutoring is one of the most consistently effective interventions available to K-12 students, but it has never been equitably distributed. In Fairfax County, as in most American school systems, personalized academic support remains largely a private good, accessible to families with the time and resources to pay for it. That gap is not static. Private tutoring has grown among wealthy families in recent decades, making public access to equivalent support increasingly vital for less advantaged students to keep pace (Nickow et al., 2024, p. 97). Without deliberate policy, the benefits of emerging AI-powered tutoring tools will follow the same pattern.

The obvious alternative, deploying general-purpose tools like ChatGPT as a tutoring layer, does not solve the problem. Teachers are already experimenting with the ChatGPT for Teachers program (Stout, 2026), and the single-vendor efficiency is genuinely appealing. But ChatGPT is hallucination-prone and creates documented academic dishonesty risks (Qadir, 2023). More fundamentally, the same tool students use to learn can be used to complete assignments entirely, and experimental evidence suggests that fully delegating tasks to general-purpose AI reduces evaluation scores by 17%, or roughly two grade levels (Shen & Tamkin, 2026, preprint). The mechanism is directly applicable to K-12 contexts: when students can bypass the learning process, skill formation suffers. As a parent of two FCPS students in the Braddock District and a technologist, I find those risks sufficient to justify restricting general-purpose GenAI in the classroom. The question is what the board does next.

Summary of Current Policy and Knowledge

Intelligent Tutoring Systems are computer-based instructional systems capable of delivering dialogue-based, personalized educational experiences. One scientifically validated example of ITS is Korbit (St-Hilaire et al., 2022, preprint). The system uses pedagogical techniques to enhance the learning experience of students at scale. If a student solution for a problem is incorrect, it will respond with "one of a dozen different pedagogical interventions to help students arrive at the correct solution" (p. 3). All of these are highly customizable and can adapt to specific curricula and lesson plans, differentiating them from traditional computerized learning experiences deployed by FCPS, such as STMath and Lexia, where the student is challenged with a problem and told if they are right or wrong (no feedback loop). In St-Hilaire et al.'s study, Korbit was set to behave as the traditional systems and compared to the "full experience." When compared, the learning gain for individual students was 92% higher, significant at the 95% confidence level. One limitation warrants acknowledgment: the study population was adult learners, so results cannot be directly extrapolated to preK-12 students.

GenAI versus ITS: A framework for classification

The fundamental policy problem is that FCPS currently lacks a classification framework that distinguishes general-purpose GenAI from purpose-built ITS. The table below makes the distinction concrete along dimensions relevant to board decision-making.

Dimension General-purpose GenAI (e.g., ChatGPT) Intelligent Tutoring Systems (e.g., Korbit)
Risk level High Hallucination-prone; academic dishonesty risk Lower Pedagogically constrained by design
Skill formation Task delegation reduces scores by ~17%; equivalent to ~2 grade levels (Shen & Tamkin, 2026) Socratic method enforces active learning; student must engage to progress
Academic integrity Same tool can be used to complete, not just support, assignments Purpose-built; cannot produce assignment answers on student's behalf
Personalization General; not curriculum-aligned by default Adaptive; aligns to specific curricula, lesson plans, and learning objectives
Privacy compliance Hallucination and data exposure risks without significant vendor guardrails FERPA/COPPA compliance built into vetting and procurement process

Policy alignment: Virginia Department of Education (VDOE)

ITS technology is actively aligned with Virginia's Department of Education (VDOE) core principles for AI integration (Virginia Department of Education, n.d.).

Do no harm

From a privacy standpoint, risks are reduced through enforced FERPA and COPPA compliance at the federal level and VCDPA compliance at the state level. The board must account for potential bias and discrimination in ITS training data when vetting solutions.

Augment, not replace humans

ITS is meant to operate as a layer between the district's teaching staff, providing oversight and direction, and the student. Teachers retain instructional authority; the system provides scalable, personalized support between sessions.

Work in partnership

Deploying ITS alongside AI researchers will bring the academic rigor necessary for this new venture, positioning FCPS as a co-awardee for federal research grants through the Institute of Education Sciences.

Policy alignment: Washington State OSPI

Washington State's Office of Superintendent for Public Instruction (OSPI) is a peer jurisdiction that has translated similar values into action. Their existing guidance for human-centered AI (Washington Office of Superintendent of Public Instruction, 2024) includes measures that, if adopted by FCPS, would directly enable ITS deployment: explicit categorization that distinguishes general-purpose GenAI from AI tutoring programs (p. 9), and defined governance roles for local education agencies in the vetting, funding, and direction of AI adoption (pp. 34–36). This distinction is critical—it would allow for the integration of ITS in FCPS while keeping tools like ChatGPT restricted. OSPI is partnering with Washington State University's AmplifyGAIN center in a pilot program aimed at the same equity and quality outcomes targeted by this brief for FCPS.

Policy Options

1

GenAI Classification

The FCPS board must establish a clear distinction between high- and low-risk AI solutions. Without this classification, any subsequent policy action on ITS lacks a principled foundation and risks conflation with existing restrictions on general-purpose AI.

2

Community Advisory Committee for AI Alignment

Institute a new district-wide, interdisciplinary, and AI-focused advisory committee to engage the technical and academic communities. The main goal will be to assist FCPS in the deployment of equity-centered AI solutions, ensuring alignment with state and federal-level guidance. An ITS pilot deployment should be the committee's first formal goal.

3

ITS Governance Framework

Establish a pre-pilot governance framework covering: compliance with federal and state-level data privacy laws and acceptable use guidance, GenAI-specific data requirements for student interaction logs, and program lifecycle design, as informed by REL Appalachia's pilot study methodology (REL Appalachia at SRI International, 2021).

4

Academic Partnership Authorization

Authorize a formal academic partnership with a local university to co-design and evaluate an equity-centered ITS pilot program, modeled on OSPI's collaboration with Washington State University. The study should target students and teachers in selected middle schools, with explicit inclusion of Title I schools.

Risk: Piloting with middle school students will require COPPA compliance for students under 13. Focusing on high school students would be less restrictive, at the expense of less impactful outcomes—tutoring impact tends to decline across grade levels (Nickow et al., 2024, p. 94), justifying the extra compliance complexity.

Recommendations

FCPS faces a dual challenge: closing the tutoring equity gap and establishing a workable, acceptable use framework for AI tools in preK-12 classrooms. Addressing both requires the board to approve all four policy options as an integrated package, in the order listed below.

  • 1
    Discrete classification of GenAI systems as a basis for determining acceptable use. Without a formal distinction between high- and low-risk AI, the committee formed in step two has no principled framework to work from, and any subsequent ITS authorization remains legally and politically exposed.
  • 2
    Community advisory committee to ensure tooling alignment and community trust. The committee's first deliverable is to operationalize the classification framework into vetting criteria, giving the governance work in step three a community-validated foundation.
  • 3
    Governance structure to support a compliant ITS program in the classroom. A pre-pilot framework covering data privacy, student interaction log requirements, and program lifecycle design is what makes it legally and institutionally possible to authorize a partner in step four.
  • 4
    Academic partnership authorization to secure subject-matter expertise and evaluate federal grant opportunities. The partner co-designs the pilot against the governance framework already in place, and positions FCPS to pursue IES funding on a competitive timeline.

This will allow the board to follow precedent from peer jurisdictions and adopt an evidence-based policy package to produce concrete action. The board must accept the underlying risks of deploying AI-enabled tools in middle school populations; the potential to deliver equitable learning gains justifies them, and FCPS has a clear model to follow.

Summary and Conclusions

Enabling ITS in the classroom will provide a pedagogical platform demonstrated to increase learning outcomes at scale. Peer jurisdictions already understand this and have taken decisive action. This is the moment for FCPS to act; further delays will deepen an equity problem already underway.

The window for action is narrowing. IES is actively funding ITS pilots through grants like AmplifyGAIN, and early-moving jurisdictions like Washington State are already positioned to shape how this technology develops in K-12 settings. FCPS has a ready-made model to follow, a strong equity rationale to lead with, and the academic partnerships available to execute responsibly. Waiting for the technology to mature further, or for other districts to move first, is not a neutral choice: it is a decision to let the tutoring gap widen while the evidence and the funding are both pointing toward action now.
